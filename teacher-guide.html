✳ little mars: storm watch

Teacher and parent guide

A 30 to 45 minute game that teaches how space weather affects astronauts, using real NASA data. For ages 7 to 12, and fine for older learners.

1. At a glance

TimeOne playthrough is about 15 to 25 minutes (five Martian days). The science check adds about 5 minutes.
NeedsA recent browser (Chrome, Edge, Safari or Firefox) with 3D graphics. No account, no install, no internet needed after the page loads. Works on phones, tablets and laptops.
Group sizeOne learner, or two learners taking turns on one screen (crew mode).
PrivacyLittle Mars does not upload player names, ages or gameplay progress to our servers. Player profiles and progress are stored locally in the browser on the device being used. Clearing browser data may erase saved progress. Only if a player chooses Live mode does the game contact NASA's DONKI service to retrieve publicly available space-weather observations. That request does not include the player's name, age or gameplay progress (NASA's server can still see the device's internet address, as any website can). The archived NASA data works without an internet connection after the game has loaded.

2. Learning goals

After playing, learners should be able to say, in their own words, that:

  • The Sun sends out flares and clouds of gas (CMEs) and fast particles, and this is called space weather.
  • Strong space weather can harm astronauts, because there is no thick air or magnetic field on Mars to block it.
  • Shielding, planning ahead and staying inside during storms reduce the risk.
  • Messages between Earth and Mars take many minutes, so crews must make decisions on their own.
  • Resources (air, food, power, time) are limited, so every choice has a cost and a trade-off.

The topics connect to everyday science themes about the Sun, energy, radiation, plants and engineering design. Check them against your own curriculum, since requirements differ between schools and countries.

3. How the game works

  1. The learner enters an age. Under 10 gets a pre-built camp, easier bonus missions, and Pip, the robot helper, gives step-by-step guidance. Age 10 and up starts with nothing built, watches a short cinematic landing, and sets up each station themselves.
  2. Five Martian days (sols) are shown on a forecast strip. Each day is a real day of space weather from NASA's DONKI database: green Calm, amber Watch, red Storm.
  3. Each sol the learner picks jobs. Every job costs power, air or daylight, and some cost Safety (radiation protection).
  4. Goals: build a storm shelter, grow and harvest food, collect three rock samples and get Earth's reply. Finish all five sols with air, food and Safety left.
  5. Missions give XP, badges and wristband levels (Dust, Copper, Silver, Gold, Aurora). Levels stay on the device across missions, and they unlock things: new modules and rover sites at Levels 2 to 8, and three expert missions at Levels 7, 9 and 10 (see section 6b).
  6. At the start of Sol 2 (after a first job is done), an optional warm-up asks four space-weather questions with no answers shown. After play, the science check asks the same four again plus one about the learner's own week, and shows before and after side by side. It is a quick self-check, not a validated test.

5. Suggested lesson plan (45 minutes)

MinutesActivity
0 to 5Ask: "What could harm astronauts on Mars that does not harm us on Earth?" Collect ideas. Introduce space weather in one sentence.
5 to 30Learners play one mission, alone or in crews of two. Walk around and ask: "Why did you choose that job first?"
30 to 38Learners take the science check, then compare their forecast strip with a neighbour's. Whose week was stormier? What did they do differently?
38 to 45Discussion (see below), then ask each learner to write one rule they would give the next crew.

Discussion questions

  • You could not do every job in a day. How did you decide what to leave out?
  • When did building the shelter early help, and what did it cost you?
  • Why would a crew on Mars need to plan more carefully than a crew on the Moon?
  • The game uses real days from NASA. What parts of the game are real, and what parts were made up? (Open "About the data" to check.)
  • Why do real mission planners watch the Sun every day?

6. What is real and what is simplified

RealThe daily record of flares, CMEs and solar particle events comes from NASA's DONKI database (Community Coordinated Modeling Center). The default archive covers 2024-01-01 to 2025-10-31 (670 daily records) and is saved inside the game. It was retrieved on 2026-11-15 with scripts/fetch_donki.py, and re-running the script gave identical data. The strength classes (A, B, C, M, X) are real.
NASA observation vs. game interpretationNASA DONKI provides real space-weather observations. Little Mars converts them into simplified Calm, Watch and Storm gameplay levels (X-class flare or solar particle event = Storm; M-class flare or a cloud of 1,500 km/s or more = Watch; anything else = Calm). These levels are educational game classifications, not official NASA astronaut-safety warnings or measured radiation levels on Mars.
Curated archiveFor gameplay balance we selected 148 five-day windows out of 666 possible ones. They avoid starting a mission with a Storm day or having consecutive Storm days, and contain one or two Storm days. The observations are real, but the selection of dates is designed for the educational game and does not represent every possible five-day period.
Gameplay adjustmentSome live NASA observations may be adjusted from Storm to Watch to keep missions playable. The original NASA observation is preserved, but the displayed gameplay classification may be modified. This is for game balance and is not a change to NASA's original scientific data. The forecast says so on the days it happens.
SimplifiedThe game pretends each day's event reaches Mars the same day. In reality, timing and direction matter. The Safety score is a teaching meter, not a radiation dose.
InventedDust storms, dust devils and cold snaps are random game events. Modules, rover sites, costs and XP values are game numbers. The 20-minute one-way radio delay is a fixed number; the real value is between about 3 and 22 minutes depending on where the planets are.
Not usedNASA's OSDR (space biology) data is not used in this version.

6b. How the score and the unlocks work

Pip's sustainability score has ten parts that add to 100: air, food and Safety (15 each), power generation (6), power storage (4), power reliability (5), life support (10), science (15), storm shelter (10) and bonus missions (5). The weights are teaching choices made by the game designers, not NASA numbers. Power is split on purpose: a battery bank only stores power and makes none, so it cannot replace solar panels, and it scores nothing if nothing charges it. Reliability measures output on the dustiest day, plus one point for using two kinds of power source. The score card in the game explains each part.

From Sol 2, three surprise events appear in a mission (a stuck buggy, a solar fault, a lost radio link, buried ice, a rockfall, a cargo capsule). Each card lists every cost before the learner chooses, and one choice always costs nothing. Good discussion prompt: "What did you give up to do that?"

What a teacher can look at afterwards

The results card has a science report: experiments done, technologies researched, sites explored, alert choices and research points. The Mission book lists extra missions and achievements. Nothing is uploaded; look at the learner's screen or ask them to read it out.

Two players: on odd sols the Engineer builds for 1 less power; on even sols the Scientist earns 1 more research point. It is turn-taking on one screen, not online play. Pip's answers are written into the game; no AI service is used.

6d. Version 4: modes, rover driving, discoveries and the colony dashboard

Status: the v4 rules are tested; many v4 screens have not yet been tried in a browser. Try the mode you plan to use before a lesson.

Modes. Choose with the Game mode button on the title screen. For a 45-minute lesson use Original Storm Watch (unchanged). Science Lab gives all eight experiments with no clock. Storm Watch Challenge is a 10-minute quiz on eight real NASA days. Free Exploration with supplies off cannot be lost and suits younger children. Story Expedition and Colony Builder last eight sols and add water; Colony Builder adds wear and repair.

Rover driving. At the Rover station choose Drive the rover. W A S D or arrows, Space to brake, E for instruments, Esc to leave. Driving is free; reaching a site and each scan cost supplies as shown. Speeds are game speeds: real rovers move slower than walking pace.

Discoveries. Each far site has a rock to scan, a ground scan and a photograph, each with a short explanation. They are kept in the Exploration journal between missions, which makes a good recap at the end of a lesson.

What is real and what is simulated in v4. Real: the NASA DONKI observations, and the kinds of feature shown (buried ice, lava tubes, river channels, layered canyons). Simulated: the rover's temperature, pressure, wind and visibility read-outs (labelled on screen), water use, module wear, and all three new experiments, each of which states the limits of its model.

NASA explorer. In Mission control, the NASA explorer tab shows any month of the archive. Useful question: "Find a Storm day. What did NASA record that made the game call it a Storm?"

New experiments, answer key. Greenhouse growth: about 14 hours of light and medium water. Thermal insulation: doubling the thickness roughly halves the heat loss. Energy storage: 2 kW for about 12 hours needs about 24 kWh, so the 25 kWh battery.

Scoring. The results card now gives a score out of 100 in seven parts, each with its reason. Outdoor work during a solar storm loses points, so speed is not rewarded over care.

Saves. Saved missions (title screen) gives three slots and an export file for moving progress between computers. The file holds game data only.

8. Accessibility

  • Controls are built to work with a keyboard. Tab moves, Enter chooses, P asks Pip, Esc skips the cinematic or closes a panel, WASD or arrow keys walk, E explores. Message cards trap Tab inside themselves, give focus to their first button and return focus when closed.
  • Text size has three steps. High contrast and reduce motion are in Settings. Sound captions write out each sound effect.
  • Pip can read messages aloud with the device's own voice.
  • Colour is never the only signal: forecast days also carry the words Calm, Watch and Storm.
  • Touch controls and buttons are at least 44 pixels. The game was checked on a phone-sized screen. An automated browser check (test-smoke.cjs) confirms every visible control has an accessible name, panels open and close, and focus handling works. It is not a substitute for a real screen reader: testing with NVDA, VoiceOver or TalkBack has not been done yet, and the 3D scene itself is not described to screen readers beyond a label. Please tell us what you find.
